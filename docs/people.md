# People

## What Pavel wants

No compromises. The world is drawn, symbolic rather than photographic, so photoreal bodies would look silly. But 3D
bodies, super beautiful — as in Disney films: Kimodo works well on them, and they are fine in form themselves.

Play with the form, with the degree of simplification. Even a far silhouette can be a 3D body filled almost black. A
3D body can be simplified — but beautiful, near and far. Beautiful means organic, natural; that doesn't contradict
cartoon simplification. Different degrees: very symbolic, or rather detailed. In any case exact: fine contours, fine
forms, a fine look in motion. No broken arms, legs, bones; real sizes of body parts.

Natural motion: no algorithm and no artist will make it — Kimodo (or a model that generates video, but that gives
video).

## Why 3D, not 2D

Kimodo gives motion in volume: the person turns, bends, walks towards the camera. A drawn 2D figure lives well in one
angle only (Live2D holds about 30 degrees of turn); every other angle has to be drawn, and the mesh starts to break.
In moood the camera moves and people turn — 2D would lose. Its strength is the artist's line and a fixed angle.

## Why the people looked bad

Not because of 3D: the bodies weren't made, they were assembled. The shape figures are tubes generated by code; Anny
is a realistic body that doesn't fit a drawn world. And real motion on a lifeless body of tubes gives the eerie
feeling — body and motion are not of one nature. In hope they are: both drawing and motion are as simple as possible,
so it breathes.

## Anny

All moood's people — woman, man, girl, teen, child, old woman, old man, toddler, smith, death — are NAVER's Anny: one
mesh (13 814 vertices) moved by her sliders. Sex, age (baby to old), height, weight, muscle, proportions, bust size and
firmness, and a mix of origins are continuous sliders 0..1; plus about 1 250 local targets made by people (nose, ears,
chin, neck, shoulders, belly, buttocks, calves…). In moood she is frozen: bodies are baked in advance by
`people-tools`, the engine gets the result. Anny's local targets are made for MakeHuman's mesh, not moood's rebuilt
one; carrying them over is a change in the baking (`bake.py`), not something to do on the side.

"What a wonderful body." One correct anatomy for every character means motion, clothes and simplification made once
for Anny serve everyone.

## How to simplify and keep beauty

- **The construction is untouchable:** bone lengths, joint places, the tilt of ribcage and pelvis, the slope of the
  shoulders — human at every level. That is what breaks in mannequins, and what lets Kimodo's motion sit right.
- **Remove by size, small to large:** veins, skin folds, separate muscles first; then fingers merge into a mitten, the
  foot becomes one form; then arms and legs become smooth tapering forms; last, the silhouette. Large masses stay.
- **Straight against curve** (Disney): one side of a limb straight, the other curved — the shin straight in front, the
  calf round behind.
- **Proportions shift a little** — head and eyes a bit bigger, legs a bit longer, hands and feet a bit smaller; the
  further from human, the worse real motion sits.
- **Check the silhouette first:** filled black, the figure still reads — pose, the gap between arm and body, the tilt
  of the head.
- **One stylisation for everything:** body, face, clothes and motion simplified alike. The simpler the body, the more
  strong poses and holds in motion.

## Simplification gives a layer of symbol

Pavel: when a body is simplified it gains symbolism. Styles: angular or round, vertical or horizontal, warm or cold,
soft or hard. In harmony with behaviour, clothes, speech, role. Adults, children and old people simplify differently.
Clothes too may change meaning with simplification — sometimes real cloth, sometimes a simpler form, sometimes the
garment itself becomes an abstraction (on a toilet sign the triangle is the dress, not the body). Geometry of bodies —
not raster tricks, not shading; "lettering" (contour, rendering) not yet.

Systems that fit this:
- **Kibbe's body types** — the balance of yin (soft, round, curved, compact) and yang (sharp, angular, vertical, wide
  in bone): dramatic, natural, classic, romantic, gamine and their soft and flamboyant variants.
- **Kitchener's essences** — dramatic, natural, classic, romantic, gamine, ingénue, ethereal; more impression and
  character than bone.
- **Jung's twelve archetypes** — innocent, everyman, hero, caregiver, explorer, rebel, lover, creator, jester, sage,
  magician, ruler; role, not body.
- **Shape language in animation** — circle: kind, soft, safe; square: steady, reliable, stubborn; triangle: dangerous,
  sharp, dynamic.

A chain: role → shape language → body type → direction of simplification → clothes, motion, speech.

The references Pavel sent ([pictures/references](pictures/references/)) show many effects that are not a function of
one part: a line of action bending the whole figure (S or C), caricature (what makes this person differ from the
average, multiplied), squash and stretch with volume kept, the silhouette as one form (pear, bean, triangle; the
wardrobe villain is one form on legs), a body part replaced by a sign. "Don't square them — make them artful."

## What we tried, and what we learned

The page of body styles ([experiments/form](../experiments/form/)): Anny reduced to a construction (one torso form along
the spine, limbs as sweeps with straight-against-curve contours, head, hands and feet as Anny's own with smooth
warps), all joined into one smooth surface; Anny's mesh carried onto it, so skeleton, skin weights and the 77 joints
stay. Sliders by meaning: genre (Pixar, Disney, anime, Burton, clay, Soviet animation, comic, UPA, rubber hose, mass on
legs, chibi, pictogram), character (princess, wardrobe villain, boss baby, tomboy, hero, teen, teacher, elf, caregiver,
sage), shape language, proportions, masses, limbs, gesture and caricature, surface; the silhouette as one form; the
sign.

The bodies became different by character and genre, but none beautiful. Why, in order of weight:
1. breadth, not depth — dozens of sliders, no body brought to the end;
2. contours taken from statistics, not designed;
3. carrying the mesh onto the form spoils the surface (lumps, rings, folds);
4. sliders are independent, and harmony is in the links between them;
5. head, hands and feet — the places the eye judges first — are the weakest;
6. pose: everyone stands like a mannequin;
7. no target to compare with.

Pavel's order: 2, 3 and 5 very well — on one body, across different style vectors; then 4, returning to 2-3-5 as
needed; only when 4 gives many good results, 1. And at every small step, several different beautiful results.

Where that stands ([pictures/form](pictures/form/)): the style as a difference between a styled and a neutral
construction, applied to the real body, made the neutral design match Anny within a few millimetres and gave clean
surfaces; simplification of small forms became an even smoothing in centimetres (Anny's mesh is twenty times finer at
a nipple, a navel or a toe than on the back). Disney, anime and Burton read smooth; Pixar, clay and Soviet are lumpy
and heavy; the head–neck seam remains; heads are blind mannequins. Still none beautiful.

## Two directions now

More in [directions.md](directions.md).


1. **The designed form is primary.** We went from the real body and removed things — a blurred real person, not a
   cartoon character. The beauty of a Disney or Pixar body comes from an invented form with a clear rhythm. So: per
   style, contours as deliberate curves; Anny only gives proportions, joints and the mesh, and is pulled onto the form.
2. **Judge beauty on a dressed person with a good head.** In games people are dressed: the player sees head, hands,
   silhouette and how clothes sit. The head is more important than the whole torso.
